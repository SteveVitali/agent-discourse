# Prose Standards (Phase 4)

What "written prose, not outline-shaped prose" means concretely, plus the
register hazards a machine-written draft must clear. Load this while drafting.

## 1. The section contract

Before writing a section you have, from the structure plan: its **function**,
**entry state**, **exit state**, **material**, and **word budget**. The contract
is that the finished section satisfies all five, and that a reader who has not
seen the plan could not reconstruct it from the prose — the scaffolding does not
show.

A section satisfies the contract when:

- **It makes a claim and does something with it.** The function is an assertion
  ("establish that legibility precedes coercion"), so the section argues, not
  surveys. A section that reports what could be said about its topic has no
  function.
- **It begins where the reader is.** The entry state is what the previous
  section left; the first sentence connects to it by content, not by
  announcement. "Having established X, we now turn to Y" is a heading in
  disguise.
- **It ends where the plan says.** The last sentence leaves the reader holding
  the exit state — and it is written to be the strongest sentence in the
  section's final third, because that is where readers stop and look up.
- **It spends its budget on the argument's hard part.** If a 900-word section
  spends 600 words on setup, the plan was wrong or the setup belongs elsewhere.
- **It uses only material the ledger permits**, at the strength the ledger
  permits.

## 2. Six tests for outline-restatement

Apply these to every drafted section; each is a defect the fresh-context audit
will look for too.

1. **The heading test.** Delete every header. Does the piece still read as a
   continuous argument, with each section's opening sentence establishing its
   own place? If the prose becomes unreadable without headers, the headers are
   carrying work the sentences should carry.
2. **The bullet-ghost test.** Are there paragraphs of the form *claim, claim,
   claim, claim* — three to six sentences of equal weight and parallel syntax
   that were bullets before they were sentences? A paragraph should have
   internal hierarchy: an assertion, its development, its complication, its
   consequence.
3. **The transition test.** Read only the first and last sentence of each
   paragraph in order. Does that sequence make an argument? If the transitions
   are all "Moreover", "Additionally", "Furthermore" — or worse, absent — the
   paragraphs are adjacent rather than connected.
4. **The specificity test.** Every abstraction in the section is cashed by at
   least one concrete instance in the section. An outline can afford to say
   "administrative subpoenas expand executive reach"; prose has to show one.
5. **The proportion test.** Section length tracks argumentative importance, not
   how much material the outline had. Outlines are longest where research was
   easiest.
6. **The re-derivation test.** Could a reader reconstruct the outline's bullet
   list from this section, one bullet per sentence? If yes, the section was
   transcribed, not written.

## 3. Sentence and paragraph discipline

Reader-expectation rhetoric, which is about comprehension rather than taste
([Gopen & Swan, *The Science of Scientific Writing*][gopen]):

- **Topic position carries old information.** Begin sentences with what the
  reader already has — the link backward — and let new material arrive later.
  Consistent old-to-new sequencing is what makes a paragraph feel like a flow
  rather than a list.
- **Stress position carries the emphasis.** Readers emphasize what arrives at
  the point of syntactic closure. Put the sentence's payload at its end; do not
  bury it before a trailing qualifier. Where the emphasis genuinely belongs
  early, restructure the sentence rather than fighting the reader.
- **Subject and verb close together.** Long interruptions between them are the
  single most common source of prose that must be reread.
- **One unit, one point** — at every scale: sentence, paragraph, section.
- **Vary the rhythm deliberately.** A short declarative after a long
  subordinate build is emphasis; three long builds in a row is fog; five short
  declaratives in a row is a drumbeat that quickly becomes a tic.

## 4. Apparatus

Apparatus is the visible machinery: headers, lists, tables, blockquotes,
footnotes, bold, and section numbers. It has a budget, set in the structure plan
from the venue's actual published work.

- **Default austerity.** If the venue's pieces don't use a device, this piece
  doesn't either. An apparatus-heavy draft at a continuous-prose venue reads as
  a report, and — in 2026 — as a machine-written report.
- **A list is for material that is genuinely enumerable**, where the reader will
  return to count the items. Everything else is prose.
- **A blockquote is for text you are about to interpret.** Quoted authority the
  draft does not then engage is decoration.
- **Bold is not emphasis** in continuous prose; syntax is. Reserve it for
  definitional first uses if the venue does that at all.

## 5. Register hazards

Readers now pattern-match machine prose, and the markers are documented: the
appearance of LLM writing assistants produced an abrupt, unprecedented rise in a
specific set of **style words** in the literature — *delves*, *showcasing*,
*underscores*, *intricate*, *notably*, *crucial*, *potential*, *findings*, and
their inflections were among the strongest excess-usage markers
([Kobak et al.][kobak]). Style, not content: exactly the vocabulary a draft
acquires for free and should be stripped of.

Treat every item below as a defect at first appearance, not a habit to moderate:

- **Marker vocabulary:** delve, showcase, underscore, intricate, notably,
  crucially, pivotal, robust, nuanced, multifaceted, tapestry, landscape (as
  metaphor), realm, testament, navigate (as metaphor), leverage (as verb),
  foster, myriad, plethora, ever-evolving, in today's world.
- **The false-antithesis tic:** "It's not X, it's Y" / "This is not merely X but
  Y." One per piece is a rhetorical move; four is a signature, and it is the
  model's signature. If a persona genuinely uses it, the voice brief sets a
  ration and the audit counts.
- **Triads.** Three-item parallel constructions everywhere, especially
  three-adjective strings. Real prose varies the count.
- **Hedge stacks:** "it could be argued that perhaps, in some sense, this may
  suggest."
- **Throat-clearing openings:** "In an era of", "As we navigate", "It is
  important to note that", "This raises the question of whether".
- **Summary paragraphs that summarize the paragraph above.** A section does not
  need to tell the reader what it just told them.
- **Aphorism inflation.** Short punchy closers on every section make none of
  them land.
- **Symmetrical both-sidesing** where the argument actually has a position, and
  the reflexive concluding move that "the truth is somewhere in between."
- **Em-dash density.** Frequent parenthetical dashes are a strong tell; convert
  most to commas, colons, or full stops, and keep the ones that do real work.
  Measure the rate; the voice brief sets the target.

## 6. What good looks like at delivery

- A reader could not tell which sections were "easy" material and which were
  hard.
- No paragraph could be cut without losing an argumentative step.
- Every abstraction has a concrete anchor within a few sentences.
- The piece's best sentences are in the stress positions of its most important
  paragraphs, not scattered.
- The register is the persona's or the declared voice's, and the marker list
  scores zero.
